How Glaciers Shape Mountain Valleys

A slow river of ice can carve granite more thoroughly than any flood. Here is how it happens, and why the evidence lasts for millions of years.

By Maria Lindqvist·12 min readUpdated

Rivers of ice

Glaciers cover about 10 percent of the land surface today, down from roughly a third during the last glacial maximum.

When snow survives the summer melt year after year, it slowly compacts into firn and then into dense glacial ice. Once the ice is thick enough, it begins to flow under its own weight, moving downhill at speeds ranging from a few centimetres to several metres per day.

Unlike a river, a glacier does not need a channel to exist. It makes its own. The ice drags rocks frozen into its base across the bedrock, scraping and polishing it like sandpaper. Geologists call this process abrasion, and the long parallel scratches it leaves behind are known as glacial striations.

How ice moves

Ice deforms like extremely stiff toothpaste. Under pressure, the crystals inside it slide past each other, a behaviour called creep. Where meltwater reaches the base, the whole glacier can also slide over its bed, which is why some glaciers speed up dramatically in early summer.

“The glacier is the most patient sculptor on Earth; it signs every valley it has ever touched.”

Creep

Crystals deform slowly under the weight of the ice above them.

Basal sliding

Meltwater lubricates the bed and lets the glacier glide.

Surges

Some glaciers advance ten times faster for a few months.

From V to U

Rivers cut narrow, V-shaped valleys because they erode mainly downward. Glaciers erode the floor and the walls at the same time, widening the valley into a U. When the ice retreats, tributary valleys are left hanging high above the main floor, often ending in spectacular waterfalls.
